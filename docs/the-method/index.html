The slow way, made survivable

A value-investing method, and the machine that keeps it honest. A prologue and 18 short chapters, for a reader who has never heard of value investing and does not know what a scheduled job is. It sells nothing. The method in it was tested and mostly did not hold, and the book says so first. If you finish it you will not know what to buy; you will know what it takes to find out.

One page: every chapter's claim in one sentence, and its three lines. Read that, then open the chapters you doubt.

How every chapter is built

The same six parts, in the same order. The claim, in one sentence. The story and the rule, with a made-up company called NAME A wherever arithmetic is needed. CENTA at this step: one real company, followed through the whole book. What the test said, where the rule was tested. Try it: one command you can run. In three lines: what to take away.

Every page has the full chapter list, a line saying where you are, and previous and next links that name the neighbouring chapters. On a chapter that belongs to a route, a third link says where that route goes next. The left and right arrow keys move between chapters. Each chapter has at most one level of fold: "the exact rule" and "where this comes from" open on demand and can be ignored. Numbered notes sit in the margin beside their sentence on a wide screen, and directly after the sentence on a phone. Nothing needs a network connection, and everything works with scripts off; a script only adds the arrow keys, the "continue" link, and the four figures you can move, in chapters 7, 10, 12 and 13. With scripts off, each of those still shows its figures.

The colours, and what each one means

Five meanings, one colour each, the same in every figure, never the only signal.

  • Value, blue: what a business is worth, and the owner's estimate of it. Square markers.
  • Price, orange: what the market quotes. Circle markers.
  • Pass, green: a test passed, a signal arrived. Tick.
  • Fail or alarm, vermillion: a test failed, a stop, an alert. Cross.
  • Missing, hatched grey: could not be checked. Question mark.
  • The owner, purple: a human decision.

Two made-up names, and one real one

Every worked example uses NAME A, and occasionally NAME B, which are fictional companies with round numbers. One real company runs through the book: Central Garden & Pet, ticker CENTA, which the owner bought and sold in October 2026. Its figures are from its own filings and his own trades, and it is an example with its mistakes left in, not a recommendation. Where another real fault is described, a company is named only where the project's public records already name it.
